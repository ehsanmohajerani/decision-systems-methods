# Case Study 01 — Technical Due Diligence With Incomplete Information

*This is a fictional example created to demonstrate technical reasoning. It is not based on a specific employer, client, property, or project.*

An owner is considering purchasing an existing commercial building for a new use. The proposed operation would require heavier equipment, interior modifications, and upgrades to several building systems.

At first glance, the property looks promising. It has been occupied for years, portions of the original drawings are available, and the seller has provided a previous engineering report.

All of that is useful.

None of it answers the question the owner actually needs answered:

**Is there enough technical confidence to commit to the property without taking on an unreasonable risk of major cost, delay, or redesign later?**

That is the decision.

It is different from asking whether the building is occupied today, whether it met the requirements in effect when it was originally constructed, or whether another engineer previously evaluated part of it.

A property can satisfy all of those conditions and still be a poor fit for the use now being considered.

## What the Existing Information Actually Tells Me

Continued occupancy tells me something about how the building has performed under its existing use and conditions.

It does not establish that the building is suitable for a materially different use.

That distinction matters.

A building may perform satisfactorily for years under one loading condition, equipment arrangement, occupancy, or operating demand and still require significant modification when those conditions change.

The available drawings are useful because they provide a starting point for understanding the original design. I would not automatically assume they represent the building exactly as it exists today.

If sheets are missing, later alterations are only partially documented, or field conditions have changed, the drawings can still be valuable without being complete.

I would treat the previous engineering report the same way.

Before relying on it, I would want to know why it was prepared, what portions of the building were evaluated, what assumptions were made, what conditions existed at the time, and whether those conditions are relevant to the proposed use.

At this point, I have information.

I do not yet have enough evidence to call the property technically acceptable or unacceptable.

That is an important difference.

## I Would Focus First on What Can Change the Decision

Incomplete information is normal in existing-building work.

By itself, it is not a reason to reject a property.

The more useful question is:

**Which missing information could materially change the acquisition decision?**

That is where I would spend the early diligence effort.

If an unresolved condition has little effect on the proposed use, resolving it immediately may add very little value.

If the uncertainty involves an area expected to support major new equipment, a critical building system, foundation behavior, available utility capacity, fire protection, access, permitting, or another potentially controlling condition, the consequence can be very different.

Those are the issues I would want to understand early because discovering them late can affect design, approvals, cost, schedule, or feasibility.

I would not try to eliminate every unknown.

I would try to identify the unknowns capable of becoming expensive.

## Assumptions Are Useful Until They Begin Acting Like Facts

Early technical evaluations require assumptions. There is nothing unusual about that.

The problem starts when a reasonable assumption quietly becomes part of the factual basis for a decision.

The existing drawings may appear to represent current construction. Previous alterations may appear to have been built as documented. Years of occupancy may suggest that existing systems have performed adequately for their present use.

Those are all reasonable starting points for preliminary analysis.

They are not automatically established facts.

If the acquisition decision depends heavily on one of them, I would want stronger support before allowing that assumption to carry the recommendation.

The consequence of being wrong matters.

An assumption that has almost no effect on the final decision deserves less attention than one that influences structural capacity, equipment layout, approval requirements, cost, and schedule at the same time.

At that point it is no longer just an assumption in the background.

It has become a decision issue.

## How I Would Treat the Previous Engineering Report

An existing engineering report can be extremely useful.

It can also create more confidence than it deserves if nobody stops to ask what the engineer was actually asked to evaluate.

My first question would be:

**What problem was that report intended to address?**

A report prepared to evaluate localized distress, review a specific alteration, investigate cracking, or support a tenant improvement may be completely sound for that purpose.

That does not mean it establishes whether the entire property is suitable for a different operation involving heavier equipment and different building demands.

This is one of the distinctions that matters most in technical due diligence:

**A credible document is not automatically an applicable document.**

I am not only interested in whether the engineer who prepared the report was competent or whether the analysis was technically sound.

I want to know whether the scope, assumptions, conditions, and conclusions of that report legitimately support the decision being made now.

If they do, I would use it.

If they do not, I would not stretch the report beyond what it was intended to establish.

## The Real Choices Are Not Simply Buy or Walk Away

One option is to proceed with the acquisition using the information already available.

That may save time and money in the short term, but it can also move unresolved technical risk directly into design, permitting, construction, and ultimately the owner’s budget.

At the other extreme, the owner could reject the property because some information is missing.

That removes the immediate uncertainty, but it may also eliminate a viable property before the important questions have actually been tested.

I would not be comfortable with either approach based solely on the facts described here.

I would favor targeted due diligence focused on the conditions most capable of changing the decision.

Depending on the property and proposed use, that could include confirming critical existing conditions, evaluating whether proposed loads and modifications are reasonably compatible with the building, reviewing the applicability of previous analyses, and identifying other building or site constraints that could have a disproportionate effect on cost or schedule.

The goal is not to prove that the property has no risk.

That is unrealistic.

The goal is to understand the material risks well enough that the owner knows what is being accepted before becoming committed to the property.

## I Would Also Look Beyond the First Discipline That Raises a Concern

Another mistake in due diligence is becoming too focused on the first technical issue that appears important.

Suppose the structural review concludes that the proposed equipment can be accommodated with manageable strengthening.

That is useful information.

It does not make the property viable if another issue becomes controlling.

A utility limitation, fire-protection requirement, access constraint, hazardous-material condition, permitting issue, operational conflict, or another building or site condition may have greater consequences than the structural work.

That does not mean one person needs to perform every discipline’s analysis.

It means the review needs enough awareness of the interfaces between disciplines to recognize when the controlling problem may sit somewhere else.

The owner ultimately cares about whether the property can support the intended use at an acceptable level of risk, cost, and schedule.

Individual technical conclusions matter because of how they affect that larger decision.

## I Would Challenge My Own Recommendation

If my preliminary recommendation is to continue with targeted diligence, I would still try to find the weaknesses in that recommendation before relying on it.

The investigation may be focused on the wrong problem.

Existing drawings may differ materially from field conditions.

The owner’s equipment or operating requirements may not yet be final.

Coordination with another discipline may expose a constraint that initially appeared secondary.

A similar building may appear to provide a useful precedent even though one important condition is different.

Experience is valuable, but similarity is not proof.

I would ask:

**What would have to be true for my current recommendation to be wrong?**

That question can be more useful than finding another reason why the recommendation is right.

If the answer identifies a condition that can reasonably be checked before acquisition and that condition could materially change the decision, I would consider resolving it before commitment.

The recommendation should become stronger because it survived challenge, not because nobody challenged it.

## Recommendation

Based on the information described in this fictional scenario, I would not characterize the property as technically acceptable or unacceptable yet.

I would recommend completing targeted technical due diligence before the owner makes an unconditional commitment.

The review should concentrate on conditions capable of materially affecting the proposed use, significant modification requirements, approval path, project cost, schedule, or overall feasibility.

If the additional work confirms that the important conditions are manageable, the owner can proceed with substantially greater technical confidence.

If it identifies a controlling constraint, the owner still has an opportunity to reconsider the property before that constraint becomes a design problem, a permitting problem, a construction problem, or an expensive ownership problem.

The timing matters.

Finding a serious issue during due diligence is inconvenient.

Finding the same issue after acquisition can be very expensive.

The purpose of additional investigation is not to prove that nothing can go wrong.

It is to understand enough of what matters before the owner becomes committed.

## When I Would Change My Recommendation

My recommendation should not become permanent simply because it was the first position taken.

I would reconsider it if new information showed that required modifications were materially greater than expected, critical existing conditions could not be reasonably verified, another building or site issue became controlling, the proposed use changed significantly, or the likely cost and schedule consequences exceeded what the owner was prepared to accept.

The opposite is equally important.

If stronger evidence confirms favorable conditions, the amount of additional investigation may reasonably decrease.

The conclusion should follow the evidence.

The evidence should not be forced to defend the original conclusion.

## Conclusion

Technical due diligence rarely requires knowing everything before a decision can be made.

It does require knowing the difference between what is established, what is assumed, and what is still uncertain enough to matter.

The objective is not to eliminate uncertainty.

It is to identify which uncertainty can actually change the decision, reduce it where the consequence justifies doing so, and understand what risk remains before committing.

That is how I would approach a major decision when the available information is incomplete: not by pretending the uncertainty is gone, and not by allowing the uncertainty itself to prevent a decision, but by determining which unknowns actually control the outcome.
